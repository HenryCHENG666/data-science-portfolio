<section id="project" class="section wrap"><div class="section-top"><p class="eyebrow">01 / FEATURED PROJECT</p><span class="status">● Completed educational prototype</span></div><h2>Virtual Cell Sandbox</h2><p class="section-lead">Can a small model predict gene-inhibition responses—and handle a dose it has never seen?</p><div class="quick-read" aria-label="Project in 30 seconds"><div><b>THE QUESTION</b><p>Can simple models predict a simulated cell response?</p></div><div><b>THE FINDING</b><p>Random forest wins at familiar doses; Ridge wins at an unseen 60% dose.</p></div><div><b>THE LIMITATION</b><p>Synthetic cells only. Real biological validity has not been tested.</p></div></div><div class="two-col"><div><h3>The idea</h3><p>A virtual cell is a computational representation used to explore cellular behavior. Here, a four-gene simulator generates paired baseline and treated cells. Ridge regression and a random forest learn to predict expression changes after inhibition.</p><p>Every test cell is held out from training. A separate 60% inhibition test asks a harder question: does the model interpolate to an unseen dose?</p><div class="tags"><span>Python</span><span>pandas</span><span>scikit-learn</span><span>Matplotlib</span><span>Grouped evaluation</span></div><a class="text-link" href="https://github.com/HenryCHENG666/data-science-project">View code, data &amp; reproducibility instructions ↗</a></div><div class="note"><span class="note-label">SCOPE &amp; DATA PERMISSIONS</span><h3>A learning model, with explicit boundaries.</h3><p>All data are generated by the included simulator. Genes, interactions and parameters are hypothetical; Growth and Stress are gene labels. These results are not evidence about real cells or drug efficacy.</p><p>Code, synthetic data and original figures are available under the MIT license. No personal or restricted data are used.</p></div></div></section>
<section id="findings" class="section tinted"><div class="wrap"><p class="eyebrow">02 / WHAT THE RESULTS SHOW</p><h2>A better familiar-dose score<br>doesn't guarantee a better new-dose model.</h2><p class="section-lead">Random forest wins on familiar doses. Ridge wins at the unseen 60% dose. Error is measured in log₂ fold-change units; lower is better.</p><figure class="figure"><img src="assets/03_model_comparison.svg" alt="Model MAE at familiar doses: no response 0.609, Ridge 0.145, random forest 0.051. At new 60 percent dose: no response 0.804, Ridge 0.130, random forest 0.230." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Same 100 held-out cell identities, two evaluation settings. One fixed seed and split; no hyperparameter search.</figcaption></figure><div class="score-table"><table><caption>Prediction error at a glance — lower MAE is better</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Evaluation</th><th scope="col">Ridge</th><th scope="col">Random forest</th><th scope="col">Lower error</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">Familiar doses</th><td>0.145</td><td>0.051</td><td>Random forest</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Unseen 60% dose</th><td>0.130</td><td>0.230</td><td>Ridge</td></tr></tbody></table></div><div class="finding-grid"><article><span class="number">01</span><h3>Interventions propagate.</h3><p>Inhibiting B lowers Growth and raises Stress, as built into the equations. This checks the simulation; it does not discover a biological mechanism.</p></article><article><span class="number">02</span><h3>Evaluation changes the story.</h3><p>Random forest MAE is 0.051 at familiar doses, but 0.230 at 60%. Ridge achieves 0.130 at 60%, about 44% lower.</p></article><article><span class="number">03</span><h3>Boundaries matter.</h3><p>All results come from one assumed simulator. Testing on real perturbation data and repeating grouped splits are essential next steps.</p></article></div></div></section>
